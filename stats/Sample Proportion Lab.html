Sample Proportion Lab

With real data we never see the true probability of anything. We only see what happened in our sample, and the proportion we observe is an estimate of the true probability. This page measures how good that estimate is: how close the sample proportion tends to land to the truth, and how that depends on the sample size.

0The question

A courier wants to know the probability p that a delivery arrives late. Nobody can tell them the true value. What they can do is check a random sample of n deliveries and count how many were late.

The fraction of late deliveries in the sample is the sample proportion p̂ (say “p-hat”). It is an estimate of p. A different sample gives a different p̂, so p̂ is itself a random variable. To judge the estimate we need its distribution: its average, its spread and its shape.

To have numbers to work with, we pretend we are nature and know the truth: p = 0.30. Then we check that what we derive matches what samples actually do.

1One delivery

Look at a single delivery. Let X = 1 if it is late and X = 0 if it is on time. A random variable with only the values 0 and 1 is called a Bernoulli random variable.

We use the definitions of the mean and variance of a discrete random variable. On the left is the general calculation, on the right the same calculation with p = 0.30.

In general E[X] = 0·(1−p) + 1·p = p E[X²] = 0²·(1−p) + 1²·p = p Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])²        = p − p² = p(1−p) SD(X) = √(p(1−p))
With p = 0.30 E[X] = 0·0.70 + 1·0.30 = 0.30 E[X²] = 0·0.70 + 1·0.30 = 0.30 Var(X) = 0.30 − 0.30²        = 0.30 − 0.09 = 0.21 SD(X) = √0.21 ≈ 0.458

Check: p(1−p) = 0.30 × 0.70 = 0.21, the same number.

2A sample of n deliveries: the binomial

Now take a sample of n deliveries, X1, X2, …, Xn. Each one is a Bernoulli variable with the same p, and we assume they are independent. The total number of late deliveries is the sum

S = X1 + X2 + … + Xn

This S is a binomial random variable. The binomial distribution models the number of successes in a fixed number of independent “yes/no” or “success/failure” trials (known as Bernoulli trials). Here a “success” is a late delivery, and the fixed number of trials is n.

For the mean and variance of S we use the rules for combining random variables: means always add, and for independent variables the variances add (the SDs do not). We use n = 50 deliveries for the numbers.

In general E[S] = p + p + … + p = np Var(S) = p(1−p) + … + p(1−p)        = np(1−p) SD(S) = √(np(1−p))
With p = 0.30, n = 50 E[S] = 50 × 0.30 = 15 late deliveries Var(S) = 50 × 0.21        = 10.5 SD(S) = √10.5 ≈ 3.24

So in a sample of 50 we expect about 15 late deliveries, give or take about 3.

3From a count to a proportion

S is a count: 15 late deliveries out of 50. But we want to estimate a probability, which is a number between 0 and 1. To turn a count into a proportion we divide by the total number of trials n. That gives the sample proportion:

p̂ = S / n     (for example 15 / 50 = 0.30)

Dividing a random variable by a constant divides its mean by that constant, and also its SD (this is transforming random variables). The variance is divided by n².

In general E[p̂] = E[S] / n = np / n = p Var(p̂) = Var(S) / n² = np(1−p) / n²         = p(1−p) / n SD(p̂) = √( p(1−p) / n )
With p = 0.30, n = 50 E[p̂] = 15 / 50 = 0.30 Var(p̂) = 10.5 / 50² = 10.5 / 2500         = 0.0042 SD(p̂) = √0.0042 ≈ 0.065

Check: SD(S)/n = 3.24 / 50 ≈ 0.065, and √(0.30 × 0.70 / 50) = √0.0042 ≈ 0.065. Both routes agree.

What this says about our estimate:

  • E[p̂] = p. On average the estimate hits the truth. It does not systematically over- or underestimate, so p̂ is an unbiased estimator.
  • The spread shrinks like 1/√n. A typical sample of 50 misses the truth by about 0.065, that is 6.5 percentage points. More data makes the estimate better, but slowly: 4 times more data only halves the error.

4The shape: the central limit theorem

Steps 1–3 give the mean and the SD of p̂ for any sample size. They say nothing about the shape of its distribution. For that we need one of the most surprising results in all of mathematics.

Central limit theorem

Take independent draws from almost any distribution with mean μ and SD σ. Average n of them. When n is large enough, the distribution of that average is approximately normal, with mean μ and SD σ/√n.

The starting distribution can be flat, lopsided, have two humps, or be just two values like our 0/1 deliveries. It does not matter: averaging washes out the original shape and leaves a bell curve. The only things that survive are the mean (it stays μ) and the spread (it becomes σ/√n). The individual draws can look like anything, yet their average always ends up looking the same. That is why the normal distribution shows up everywhere in nature and in data.

See it for yourself. Pick a starting distribution, then raise n from 1 and watch the right-hand histogram turn into a bell.

What this means for p̂

p̂ = S/n = (X1 + … + Xn)/n is exactly an average of n Bernoulli values, the “Yes/no” case above. So the central limit theorem applies: for large enough n, p̂ is approximately normal with mean p and SD √(p(1−p)/n). Those are the numbers from steps 1–3.

“Large enough” depends on p. When p is close to 0 or 1, almost all values are the same and the starting distribution is very lopsided, so we need a bigger sample before the bell appears. The usual rule of thumb is the normal condition: expect at least 10 successes and at least 10 failures.

np ≥ 10    and    n(1−p) ≥ 10

5Try this, and write down what you see

  1. A tiny sample. Set p = 0.30 and n = 5. Which values can p̂ take? (Hint: k/5.) Does the histogram look like the normal curve? Is the normal condition met?
  2. Grow the sample. Keep p = 0.30 and move n to 25, then 100, then 400. Write down the simulated SD each time. Describe what happens to the shape and to the width of the histogram.
  3. The √n law. Compare your SDs for n = 25 and n = 100. The sample is 4 times larger. By what factor did the SD shrink? Does it match √4 = 2? Do the same for n = 100 and n = 400.
  4. Randomness of the simulation. Keep the settings fixed and press “Draw 2,000 new samples” a few times. Which numbers change and which stay fixed? Why is the theory column never changing?
  5. The centre. In every setting, compare the average of the 2,000 estimates with p. What does this tell you about whether p̂ is a fair (unbiased) estimate?
  6. When the normal curve fails. Set p = 0.05 and n = 40. The condition np ≥ 10 fails. Describe the shape. Set c = 0.10 and compare the simulated P(p̂ > c) with the normal-curve value. Then raise n to 300 and compare again.
  7. Reading a probability. Set p = 0.30, n = 400 and c = 0.34. Write down the z-score. Sketch the normal curve, shade the area to the right of z, and explain why the probability is 1 − P(Z < z). Check that the normal-curve value is close to the simulated share.

6A worked example

The courier’s true late rate is p = 0.30. A manager checks n = 525 deliveries. How likely is it that the sample shows more than 34% late, P(p̂ > 0.34)?

Reasoning Condition: np ≥ 10, n(1−p) ≥ 10 Centre: E[p̂] = p Spread: SD(p̂) = √(p(1−p)/n) z = (c − p) / SD(p̂) P(p̂ > c) ≈ 1 − P(Z < z)
Numbers 157.5 ≥ 10 and 367.5 ≥ 10 ✓ E[p̂] = 0.30 √(0.30 × 0.70 / 525) = √0.0004 = 0.02 z = (0.34 − 0.30) / 0.02 = 2.00 1 − 0.9772 = 0.0228

Read it back as a statement about the estimate: with 525 deliveries, an estimate that is 4 percentage points too high happens in only about 2% of samples. That is how well this estimate works.
